# Uncertainty, Calibration, and Selective Prediction for Pair-Order Consistent Semantic Change Detection

**Scope note.** This brief targets a Siamese CNN that outputs a per-pixel binary "changed / unchanged" probability map from a reference–edited RGB frame pair, trained with weighted BCE + Dice on free Colab/Kaggle GPUs over one semester. Changed pixels are ~2–5% of the frame. The product requirement is a *human-in-the-loop reviewer aid*: it must (1) report calibrated confidence, (2) support **image/pair-level abstention** ("defer this pair to a human"), and (3) document, with evidence (reliability diagrams, risk–coverage curves), when it should defer. Everything below is organized so the team can lift formulas and citations directly into the report's methodology section.

---

## 1. Uncertainty-quantification methods feasible on a free GPU in one semester

### 1.1 Background: aleatoric vs. epistemic

Kendall & Gal (NeurIPS 2017) formalize the split that the rest of this brief relies on: **aleatoric** uncertainty is irreducible noise in the data (sensor noise, illumination/nuisance changes between the two frames, registration jitter) and **epistemic** uncertainty is model uncertainty that shrinks with more/better training data. They show that combining a heteroscedastic aleatoric term with an epistemic-uncertainty method (MC dropout) outperforms either alone on per-pixel semantic segmentation and depth regression, and that explicit aleatoric modelling acts as **learned loss attenuation** — it automatically down-weights the loss on noisy/ambiguous pixels ("What Uncertainties Do We Need in Bayesian Deep Learning for Computer Vision?", NeurIPS 2017, arXiv:1703.04977). This distinction matters directly for the project: nuisance shifts (lighting, shadows, viewpoint, compression artifacts) between reference and edited frames are largely an **aleatoric** phenomenon — they are inherent to the pair, not fixable by more training data — so a pure epistemic method (MC Dropout/ensembles trained only on clean data) will systematically under-flag nuisance-confused pixels unless paired with an aleatoric signal or with post-hoc calibration fit on nuisance-heavy validation data (see §4).

### 1.2 Method-by-method summary

**Monte Carlo Dropout** (Gal & Ghahramani, ICML 2016, PMLR v48; applied to segmentation as *Bayesian SegNet*, Kendall/Badrinarayanan/Cipolla, BMVC 2017, arXiv:1511.02680). Keep dropout active at test time and run $T$ stochastic forward passes ($T{=}10$–$30$ is typical):
$$\hat p(x) = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T p_t(x), \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}[p(x)] = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T p_t(x)^2 - \hat p(x)^2$$
Bayesian SegNet reports MC-dropout uncertainty *improves* mIoU by 2–3% (better decision boundary via averaging) in addition to giving a usable per-pixel variance map. Cost: requires dropout layers in the decoder (cheap to add to a U-Net-style Siamese decoder); extra inference cost is $T\times$ forward passes (10–30× latency, still fine on Colab for offline batch review). Training cost: zero beyond standard dropout regularization. Implementation difficulty: **1–2/5** (add `nn.Dropout2d`, keep `model.train()` at inference for dropout layers only, average). This is the single best cost/benefit method for this project.

**Deep Ensembles** (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, Blundell, NeurIPS 2017). Train $M{=}3$–$5$ independently-initialized (optionally differently-seeded/bootstrapped) copies of the full network; average softmax/sigmoid outputs for the predictive mean, use inter-member variance/disagreement as epistemic uncertainty. Lakshminarayanan et al. show ensembles are as well- or better-calibrated than MC-dropout/Bayesian NNs and degrade more gracefully under distribution shift — a result reconfirmed and extended by Ovadia et al. (§4). Cost: $M\times$ full training time and $M\times$ storage/inference — this is the expensive one on a free-GPU budget, but $M{=}3$ small U-Nets is usually still feasible in a semester if each run is a few hours. Difficulty: **1/5** conceptually, but budget-heavy.

**Cheap ensemble variants**:
- *Snapshot Ensembles* (Huang et al., ICLR 2017, "Train 1, get $M$ for free", arXiv:1704.00109): use a cyclic LR schedule, save a checkpoint at the bottom of each cycle, ensemble the last $m$ snapshots. Gets ensemble-like diversity for **~1×** training cost (only inference cost multiplies by $m$). Difficulty: **2/5** (implement cosine-cyclic LR + checkpoint saving). Good match for a compute-constrained semester project — recommended over full deep ensembles if GPU time on Colab/Kaggle is the binding constraint.
- *BatchEnsemble* (Wen et al., ICLR 2020, arXiv:2002.06715): each ensemble member's weight matrix is a shared matrix Hadamard-producted with a cheap rank-1 per-member factor ($W_i = W \circ (r_i s_i^\top)$); all members' forward passes batch together on one GPU. Reduces ensemble memory/compute to near single-model cost. Difficulty: **3/5** (needs custom layer wrapping, less plug-and-play in vanilla PyTorch than dropout).
- *Multi-head / shared-trunk ensembles*: one shared encoder, $K$ independent decoder heads with different init/dropout masks, trained jointly. Cheapest of the "ensemble-like" options for a segmentation decoder; difficulty **2/5**; less diversity than independent full ensembles because the trunk is shared, so uncertainty estimates are a weaker signal for genuine epistemic (encoder-level) uncertainty — acceptable as a stretch/backup, not a first choice.

**Test-Time Augmentation (TTA)** (Wang et al., "Aleatoric uncertainty estimation with test-time augmentation for medical image segmentation," Neurocomputing 2019, arXiv:1807.07356). Apply $T$ stochastic test-time transforms (flips, small rotations, brightness/contrast jitter mimicking nuisance shift) to the *pair*, run inference on each, invert the transform on the output masks, and treat the spread across predictions as aleatoric uncertainty. Wang et al. show TTA-based aleatoric uncertainty is a *better* uncertainty signal than test-time dropout alone and reduces overconfident wrong predictions, and it composes cleanly with MC Dropout (MC-Dropout ⊗ TTA gives a combined aleatoric+epistemic estimate at $T_{\text{drop}}\times T_{\text{aug}}$ inference cost). Zero extra training cost; inference cost scales with number of augmentations (5–10 is typical). Difficulty: **1/5**. Because the project's core failure mode is nuisance-shift-induced false positives, **TTA is arguably the single highest-value cheap addition** — the augmentation set should specifically include the nuisance transforms the project already needs to be robust to (brightness, JPEG-like compression, small warps), turning the robustness augmentation pipeline into a free uncertainty signal.

**Temperature scaling** (Guo, Pleiss, Sun, Weinberger, ICML 2017, arXiv:1706.04599). Post-hoc, one learned scalar $T$ applied to logits before the sigmoid/softmax:
$$\hat p = \sigma(z/T)$$
$T$ is fit by minimizing NLL on a held-out validation set with the base network frozen (a 1-D convex optimization, converges in seconds). Guo et al. show it is "surprisingly effective," does not change accuracy or the argmax decision, and is far cheaper than Platt scaling or isotonic regression, but it is a *single global scalar* — it cannot fix per-region or per-class miscalibration (e.g., different calibration needed on changed vs. background pixels) and cannot fix miscalibration under **distribution shift** without being refit (§4). Zero training cost, zero extra inference cost (one multiply). Difficulty: **1/5**. This is the mandatory minimum post-hoc step for the project regardless of what else is used.

**Evidential Deep Learning / Dirichlet** (Sensoy, Kaplan, Kandemir, NeurIPS 2018, "Evidential Deep Learning to Quantify Classification Uncertainty"). Replace the softmax head with a non-negative "evidence" head (e.g. ReLU/Softplus output) $e_k \ge 0$ per class; set Dirichlet parameters $\alpha_k = e_k + 1$, total evidence $S=\sum_k \alpha_k$, belief mass $b_k = e_k/S$, and **vacuity/uncertainty mass** $u = K/S$ (for binary segmentation $K{=}2$). The loss is the expected sum-of-squares error under the Dirichlet plus a KL term that drives evidence for the *wrong* class toward zero:
$$\mathcal L_i = \sum_{k=1}^K \big(y_{ik}-\hat p_{ik}\big)^2 + \hat p_{ik}(1-\hat p_{ik})/(\alpha_{ik,\text{tot}}+1) \;+\; \lambda_t\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathrm{Dir}(\tilde\alpha_i)\,\|\,\mathrm{Dir}(\mathbf 1)\right)$$
Gives uncertainty in a **single forward pass** (no MC sampling, no ensemble), which is attractive for a compute-constrained project. Downsides for this specific use case: (a) it changes the loss/training objective (interacts with the planned weighted-BCE+Dice — needs its own re-tuning of the imbalance weighting, since the evidential loss no longer looks like standard BCE), (b) uncertainty is per-pixel by construction so it must be aggregated to a per-pair score for the gate, and (c) published segmentation adaptations are thinner than for classification (see "Mutual Evidential Deep Learning for Medical Image Segmentation," arXiv:2505.12418, for a segmentation-specific variant, and note recent work questions whether EDL's vacuity term is a *faithful* epistemic-uncertainty estimate at all — "Is Epistemic Uncertainty Faithfully Represented by Evidential Deep Learning Methods?", arXiv:2402.09056). Difficulty: **4/5** (new head, new loss, re-tune imbalance handling). Treat as a **stretch goal**, not the core stack.

**Heteroscedastic aleatoric loss** (Kendall & Gal, NeurIPS 2017). Add a second output head predicting a per-pixel log-variance $s_i=\log\sigma_i^2$; corrupt the logits with learned Gaussian noise before the sigmoid and approximate the expectation by Monte-Carlo sampling $T$ noisy logits at train time only:
$$\hat x_{i,t} = f_i(x) + \sigma_i \cdot \epsilon_t,\ \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1), \qquad \mathcal L_i \approx \log\frac1T\sum_t \exp\!\big(\hat x_{i,t,c^*} - \log\!\sum_c e^{\hat x_{i,t,c}}\big)$$
(For binary segmentation this collapses to sampling noisy logits into the BCE.) This is trained end-to-end at essentially the cost of the extra head (cheap) and needs **zero extra inference passes** at test time (unlike MC Dropout/TTA) — you can take $\sigma_i$ directly as a per-pixel aleatoric confidence map in one forward pass, which is attractive under a tight Colab GPU-hours budget. Difficulty: **3/5** (new head + Monte-Carlo loss, some tuning of $T$ and the noise-sampling code; needs care that the extra head does not destabilize the Dice term).

**SWAG** (Maddox et al., NeurIPS 2019, arXiv:1902.02476). Continue training with a constant/cyclic LR after convergence, track a running mean $\bar\theta$ (the SWA solution) and a low-rank-plus-diagonal covariance from the SGD iterate deviations, giving an approximate Gaussian posterior $q(\theta)=\mathcal N(\bar\theta, \Sigma_{\text{diag}}+\Sigma_{\text{low-rank}})$ that is sampled $T$ times at test time like an ensemble. Maddox et al. show it matches or beats MC-Dropout/K-FAC-Laplace/temperature-scaling on calibration and OOD detection, with training cost = a short extra fine-tuning phase and inference cost = $T\times$ forward passes (like an ensemble but from *one* training run). Library: `github.com/wjmaddox/swa_gaussian` (research code, not a maintained package). Difficulty: **3/5**. Reasonable stretch goal if MC-Dropout alone underperforms.

**Laplace approximation** (Daxberger et al., "Laplace Redux — Effortless Bayesian Deep Learning," NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2106.14806; library **`laplace-torch`**, `aleximmer.github.io/Laplace`). Fit a Gaussian $\mathcal N(\theta_{MAP}, H^{-1})$ around the already-trained MAP weights using a Generalized-Gauss-Newton/Fisher approximation to the Hessian $H$; the library supports **last-layer-only** Laplace, which is cheap (only the final conv/linear layer's Hessian, a few seconds to a minute post-hoc) versus full-network Laplace (expensive, generally infeasible on a free GPU for anything beyond small last-layer treatment). This is a genuinely "bolt it on after training" option: zero change to the training loop, apply once training is done. Caveat: `laplace-torch`'s primary interfaces target classification/regression heads; dense per-pixel segmentation is not a first-class use case in the library, so plan on adapting the last conv layer as a per-pixel linear head and expect implementation friction. Difficulty: **4/5** for the segmentation adaptation despite the library existing. Reasonable stretch goal, not MVP.

## 2. Calibration metrics for segmentation under extreme class imbalance

### 2.1 Standard definitions

**Expected Calibration Error (ECE)** (Guo et al. 2017; Naeini et al. 2015 for the classification version): partition confidences into $M$ equal-width bins $B_m$, compare bin-average confidence to bin-average accuracy, weighted by bin occupancy:
$$\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\big|\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\big|$$
**Maximum Calibration Error (MCE)** replaces the sum with a max. **Adaptive/equal-mass binning** (quantile bins with equal sample counts, as opposed to equal-width bins) is recommended by Nixon et al. ("Measuring Calibration in Deep Learning," CVPR-W 2019, arXiv:1904.01685): they show adaptive binning gives far more stable rank-ordering of methods as the bin count $M$ varies than fixed-width binning, and propose **Adaptive/Thresholded Adaptive Calibration Error (ACE/TACE)** and using the L2 norm over L1 for both optimization and evaluation stability. **Class-wise ECE** computes ECE separately per class (here: separately for the "changed" and "unchanged" class confidences) and averages — this is the key remedy for imbalance, discussed next.

**Brier score**: $\mathrm{BS} = \frac1N\sum_i (\hat p_i - y_i)^2$ (binary) or $\frac1N\sum_i\sum_k(\hat p_{ik}-y_{ik})^2$ (multiclass). Murphy's (1973) decomposition is directly useful for diagnosing *why* a model is miscalibrated:
$$\mathrm{BS} = \underbrace{\mathrm{Reliability}}_{\text{miscalibration}} - \underbrace{\mathrm{Resolution}}_{\text{discrimination}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Uncertainty}}_{\text{base-rate variance}}$$
Under 2–5% positive-pixel prevalence, the "Uncertainty" term (variance of the base rate) is tiny by construction, so a model that just predicts the base rate everywhere gets a deceptively good Brier score too — Brier alone, like ECE, needs the imbalance-aware treatment below.

**NLL**: $\mathrm{NLL} = -\frac1N\sum_i \log \hat p_i(y_i)$, proper scoring rule, sensitive to confident wrong predictions (heavily penalizes a confident false negative on a rare changed pixel) — useful as a secondary/monitoring metric but on its own also gets dominated by the easy background majority.

### 2.2 Why naive pixel-ECE is misleading under extreme imbalance — direct answer to "is ECE computed over all pixels or a subset in the CD literature?"

This is a documented, named failure mode, not a hypothetical: a 2026 preprint on post-hoc segmentation calibration explicitly reports that on a severely imbalanced medical segmentation benchmark (BraTS), "the voxel-wise NLL objective and ECE are dominated by easy background voxels" while calibration on the rare foreground class remains poor ("Rethinking Post-Hoc Calibration in Semantic Segmentation," arXiv:2607.01902). Mechanically: with 95–98% of pixels being easy true-negative "unchanged" pixels predicted with high, *correct* confidence, the vast majority of bins in a naive all-pixel ECE are dominated by well-calibrated background — a model that is badly miscalibrated on the rare changed-pixel class (e.g., systematically overconfident on nuisance-shift false positives) can still show a near-zero whole-frame ECE, because the bin-occupancy weighting in the ECE formula (the $|B_m|/n$ term) gives background pixels overwhelming weight. This is the exact same imbalance pathology that motivates weighted BCE/Dice in the loss — it needs an analogous fix in the *metric*.

**Practical protocol for this project** (synthesizing Mehrtash et al. TMI 2020, Nixon et al. CVPR-W 2019, and arXiv:2607.01902's remedies):
1. Report **class-wise ECE** — ECE computed separately conditioned on ground-truth changed vs. unchanged pixels, or on predicted-positive vs. predicted-negative pixels — never only the pooled all-pixel number.
2. Report an **"ACE-style" bin-uniform variant**: average the calibration gap uniformly over non-empty bins rather than weighting by occupancy (arXiv:2607.01902's Average Calibration Error, and Nixon et al.'s adaptive/equal-mass binning), so rare-but-important confidence ranges are not washed out.
3. Optionally restrict one reported ECE to **foreground pixels only** (ground-truth changed pixels, or predicted-changed pixels) — this directly answers "over all pixels or a subset": **the literature's answer, once imbalance is taken seriously, is "not naive all-pixel ECE — compute it per-class or restricted to the foreground, and report both."**
4. Report the Brier decomposition's Reliability term specifically (not just total Brier) since Resolution/Uncertainty terms are themselves imbalance-skewed.
5. If boundary quality matters to the reviewer workflow, consider **Boundary-Aware ECE** (distance-to-boundary weighted), also proposed in arXiv:2607.01902, since change-mask boundaries are exactly where a human reviewer's eye goes first.

## 3. Selective prediction / risk–coverage for dense prediction

### 3.1 Formal definitions

For a classifier $f$ with a confidence/selection function $g(x)$ and loss $\ell$, at threshold $\tau$:
$$\text{Coverage: } \phi(\tau) = \Pr[g(x)\ge\tau], \qquad \text{Selective risk: } r(\tau) = \frac{\mathbb E\big[\ell(f(x),y)\cdot\mathbf 1[g(x)\ge\tau]\big]}{\phi(\tau)}$$
The **risk–coverage (RC) curve** plots $r$ against $\phi$ as $\tau$ sweeps from the strictest to the most permissive value (Geifman & El-Yaniv, "Selective Classification for Deep Neural Networks," NeurIPS 2017). Empirically, sort $N$ samples by descending confidence (permutation $\sigma$), then at coverage $k/N$:
$$r(k/N) = \frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k \mathbf 1[\hat y_{\sigma(i)}\ne y_{\sigma(i)}]$$
**AURC** $=\int_0^1 r(\phi)\,d\phi \approx \frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N r(k/N)$ — lower is better; a perfect confidence ranking (correct predictions all ranked above incorrect ones) achieves the theoretical minimum. **E-AURC** (Geifman, Uziel & El-Yaniv, "Bias-Reduced Uncertainty Estimation for Deep Neural Classifiers," ICLR 2019) is *excess* AURC over that oracle minimum, $\mathrm{E\text{-}AURC} = \mathrm{AURC} - \mathrm{AURC}^\*$, which isolates how much of the RC curve's area is attributable to *imperfect ranking by the confidence score* rather than to the model's irreducible base error rate — the more informative number when comparing confidence estimators across models of different baseline accuracy.

### 3.2 Per-pixel vs. per-image/per-pair rejection — this is the crux design decision for the project

Segmentation selective prediction is not a solved special case of classification selective prediction; the unit of abstention must be chosen deliberately. Recent work frames exactly this tradeoff: pixel-level deferral is the most granular (defer only the actually-ambiguous pixels) but "produces fragmented deferral maps that may be impractical for human review," while image-level deferral is clean for a review workflow but "discards good predictions along with bad ones" ("Rethinking Uncertainty in Segmentation: From Estimation to Decision," arXiv:2604.13262 — note: a 2026 preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, but its problem framing is directly usable). That paper formalizes three concrete pixel-level deferral policies worth knowing even though the project's target is pair-level:
- **Global threshold**: $\delta_{ij} = \mathbf 1[u_{ij}\le\tau]$, one $\tau$ for the whole dataset.
- **Image-adaptive threshold**: $\delta_{ij} = \mathbf 1[u_{ij}\le Q_\alpha(\{u_{ij}\}_{\text{image }n})]$ — a per-image percentile of the uncertainty map, robust to scenes with globally different difficulty.
- **Confidence-aware deferral**: $s_{ij}=u_{ij}\cdot(1-c_{ij})$ with $c_{ij}=2|\hat p_{ij}-0.5|$ (distance from the decision boundary), thresholding the *product* of uncertainty and boundary-proximity rather than uncertainty alone.

**Recommended pair-level aggregation for this project** (since the deliverable is a per-pair reviewer decision, "does this pair need human eyes," not a pixel-fragmented mask): reduce the pixel-level uncertainty/confidence map $u\in\mathbb R^{H\times W}$ to one scalar pair-level score $g(\text{pair})$ before applying the RC/AURC machinery at the pair level. Reasonable, cheap choices, roughly in order of sophistication:
1. **Max-softmax-response (MSR) aggregate** (Hendrycks & Gimpel, ICLR 2017, "A Baseline for Detecting Misclassified and OOD Examples," arXiv:1610.02136): $g = \text{mean or top-}k\text{-percentile of } \max(\hat p_{ij}, 1-\hat p_{ij})$ over predicted-positive pixels. Zero extra cost, use as the mandatory baseline every other confidence estimator must beat.
2. **Predicted-changed-pixel-count-weighted mean confidence**: average confidence over only the pixels the model calls "changed" (the region a reviewer would actually look at), which better reflects "how sure is the model about the change region it flagged" than an unweighted whole-frame average (whole-frame averages get swamped by confident background, same imbalance pathology as §2).
3. **Soft-Dice-Confidence (SDC)**: a purpose-built, near-optimal per-image confidence estimator *for selective prediction in semantic segmentation* — "Soft Dice Confidence: A Near-Optimal Confidence Estimator for Selective Prediction in Semantic Segmentation" (arXiv:2402.10665) derives a closed-form, linear-time approximation to the expected Dice score of a prediction from the pixel-wise probabilities themselves, and shows it is a better ranking signal for image-level accept/defer decisions than naive confidence averaging. This is the most directly relevant single citation for the project's stated deliverable ("image-level false-alert rate") and is worth implementing as the primary pair-level score, with MSR-aggregate as the comparison baseline.
4. **Energy score** (Liu, Wang, Owens, Li, NeurIPS 2020, "Energy-based Out-of-distribution Detection," arXiv:2010.03759... commonly cited as NeurIPS 2020): $E(x) = -\log\sum_c e^{z_c}$ from the pre-sigmoid logits, aggregated per-pair the same way as (1)/(2); reported to separate in-/out-of-distribution examples better than softmax-based scores and is essentially free to compute from existing logits. Useful as a second, cheap signal specifically for OOD-like nuisance shifts (§4), complementary to MSR.
5. **Epistemic disagreement from §1** (MC-Dropout variance / ensemble disagreement / TTA spread), aggregated the same way — this is the signal that specifically targets model uncertainty rather than just decision-boundary proximity, and is the one that should improve most under distribution shift (§4).

### 3.3 Trainable alternatives: SelectiveNet and Deep Gamblers

**SelectiveNet** (Geifman & El-Yaniv, ICML 2019, PMLR v97, arXiv:1901.09192): a three-headed network (prediction head, selection head $g$, auxiliary head) trained end-to-end with a selective loss that targets a *specified* coverage $c$ via a constrained/interior-point formulation, jointly optimizing prediction and rejection rather than thresholding a post-hoc confidence score. Reports state-of-the-art RC trade-offs vs. post-hoc thresholding. Cost for this project: requires architecture surgery (extra head + a coverage-constrained loss term) and a full retrain, plus its own hyperparameter (target coverage) that would need tuning per deployment scenario. Given the project already has a fixed loss (weighted BCE+Dice) and a tight compute budget, SelectiveNet is a **stretch goal**, not MVP — the post-hoc confidence-threshold route (§3.2) gets 80% of the benefit for a fraction of the engineering cost and doesn't require re-architecting the segmentation network.

**Deep Gamblers** (Ziyin et al., NeurIPS 2019, "Learning to Abstain with Portfolio Theory," arXiv:1907.00208): adds a $(K{+}1)$-th "abstain" output class trained with a portfolio-theory-derived loss (maximizing a doubling rate) so the network learns to output an explicit abstain probability; reviewers of the original paper specifically praised it for being simple enough to try immediately (no retraining loop changes, no architecture surgery beyond one extra output unit) end-to-end trainable, no sampling or ensembling required. For binary segmentation this maps to a 3-way per-pixel softmax (changed / unchanged / abstain) instead of a sigmoid — a moderate but not huge change to the output head and loss, more tractable than SelectiveNet for a semester project if a *trained* (rather than *post-hoc*) abstention mechanism is wanted. Rate as difficulty **3/5**, reasonable stretch goal.

### 3.4 Selecting an operating point on validation data

Because coverage/risk trade off monotonically along the RC curve, the concrete procedure is: (1) compute the pair-level confidence score for every validation pair, (2) sort descending, (3) sweep the threshold $\tau$ and compute selective risk (e.g., 1 − pair-level Dice/IoU, or a human-relevant proxy like "did we miss a real change region") and coverage at each $\tau$, (4) pick $\tau^\*$ either as the point achieving a target selective risk (e.g., "risk ≤ 5% missed-change rate on accepted pairs") or a target coverage (e.g., "auto-accept at most 70% of pairs, defer the rest"), whichever framing matches the reviewer-time-savings argument the project wants to make; (5) **never tune $\tau$ on the test set** — this must be fixed on a held-out validation split, exactly analogous to (and should reuse the same split logic as) threshold selection in §7.

## 4. Calibration under distribution shift (train on curated imagery, test on video frames)

**Ovadia et al., "Can You Trust Your Model's Uncertainty? Evaluating Predictive Uncertainty Under Dataset Shift," NeurIPS 2019 (arXiv:1906.02530)** is the single most load-bearing citation for this section of the report, because it is a large-scale, controlled study of exactly the failure mode the project is worried about (train-time distribution ≠ test-time distribution). Key findings to cite directly:
- Under increasing dataset shift, **every** method's calibration degrades — there is no free lunch — but the *rate* of degradation differs sharply across methods.
- **Deep ensembles were the most robust** of the methods tested (more so than MC-Dropout, temperature scaling alone, or single-model variational/Bayesian methods), maintaining better-calibrated, appropriately higher-entropy predictions as shift increased.
- **Temperature scaling fit on in-distribution validation data does not transfer**: a $T$ calibrated on clean validation data under-corrects (or even miscorrects) confidence once the test distribution shifts, because $T$ is a single global scalar tuned to one operating regime.
- Post-hoc calibration is still useful under shift, but **only if it is fit on data that resembles the shifted regime** — i.e., if the team has *any* nuisance-shifted / video-frame-like validation pairs (even a small hand-labeled set), fitting temperature scaling (or a class-wise variant) on *that* split rather than on the clean train-distribution validation split will transfer far better to the true video-frame test conditions.
- No method tested gives calibrated uncertainty under *large* shift "for free" — this is the empirical grounding for why the project's report should frame calibration numbers as conditional on shift severity, and should explicitly test on a held-out slice that is more nuisance-shift-heavy than the training distribution rather than only reporting an i.i.d. validation number.

**Practical implications for the project, directly derived from the above:**
1. If GPU budget allows even a cheap ensemble (snapshot ensembles are the affordable proxy — §1.2), prefer it over a single calibrated model specifically *because* the shift-robustness gap is the largest reported effect in Ovadia et al., not because of raw in-distribution calibration numbers (where the gap between methods is smaller).
2. Collect or hold out a small labeled "shift validation" set that looks like the real video-frame test conditions (different from the main train/val split) and **refit temperature scaling on it**, not on the in-distribution validation split — this single change is cheap (seconds of compute) and directly targeted at the paper's main actionable finding.
3. Report calibration (ECE/Brier, §2) and risk–coverage (§3) **separately for the in-distribution validation split and the shift/video split** — a single pooled number will hide exactly the degradation Ovadia et al. document, and a two-column comparison is itself a strong, citable piece of evidence for the report's "when should the detector defer" narrative.
4. Conformal methods (§5) are the one class of technique in this space that keeps a *distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantee* even when calibration in the classical (ECE) sense is not achievable — but the guarantee is only valid for calibration and test data drawn from the same distribution (exchangeability), so it degrades under shift too, just with an explicit, quantifiable failure mode rather than a silent one. Flag this honestly in the report rather than overclaiming conformal robustness to shift.

## 5. Conformal prediction for segmentation

### 5.1 Core recipe: Risk-Controlling Prediction Sets (RCPS)

**Bates, Angelopoulos, Lei, Malik, Jordan, "Distribution-Free, Risk-Controlling Prediction Sets," J. ACM 2021 (arXiv:2101.02703)**: given a black-box predictor and a bounded, monotone loss function, use a small held-out **calibration set** to choose the single free parameter of a nested family of prediction sets (e.g., a probability threshold, or a mask-shrinkage amount) such that, with probability $\ge 1-\delta$ over the draw of the calibration set, the expected loss on a *future* test point is $\le\alpha$:
$$\Pr_{\text{cal. set}}\big[R(\hat\lambda) \le \alpha\big] \ge 1-\delta$$
The guarantee is delivered via a concentration inequality (Hoeffding or the tighter Bentkus bound) applied to the empirical calibration-set risk $\hat R_n(\lambda)$, giving an upper confidence bound $\widehat{\mathrm{UCB}}(\lambda)$, and picking $\hat\lambda = \inf\{\lambda: \widehat{\mathrm{UCB}}(\lambda')\le\alpha\ \forall \lambda'\ge\lambda\}$ (the least conservative $\lambda$ that still keeps the *upper bound* on risk below $\alpha$, not just the point estimate — this is what makes the guarantee finite-sample rather than asymptotic).

**Conformal Risk Control** (Angelopoulos, Bates, Fisch, Lei, Schuster, arXiv:2208.02814, ICLR 2024) generalizes/simplifies this to any bounded, monotone loss, with a simpler closed-form calibration rule (no explicit concentration-bound machinery needed at calibration time, at the cost of an in-expectation rather than high-probability guarantee):
$$\hat\lambda = \inf\left\{\lambda:\ \frac{n}{n+1}\hat R_n(\lambda) + \frac{1}{n+1} \le \alpha\right\}$$
where $\hat R_n(\lambda)$ is the empirical risk on $n$ calibration examples at threshold/shrinkage parameter $\lambda$. This guarantees $\mathbb E[R(\hat\lambda)]\le\alpha$ over a fresh test point, assuming exchangeability between calibration and test data (the same caveat flagged in §4 re: distribution shift).

### 5.2 Segmentation-specific instantiations (directly reusable)

- **Mossina, Dalmau, Andéol, "Conformal Semantic Image Segmentation: Post-hoc Quantification of Predictive Uncertainty," CVPR Workshops 2024**: applies Conformal Risk Control to multi-class semantic segmentation (tested on Cityscapes, ADE20K, and — notably — the aerial-imagery dataset LoveDA), producing per-pixel prediction sets with a distribution-free guarantee on a chosen risk (e.g., a miss-rate on the class of interest).
- **Mossina & Friedrich, "Conformal Prediction for Image Segmentation Using Morphological Prediction Sets," MICCAI 2025 (arXiv:2503.05618)**: specifically for *binary* segmentation, constructs a nested family of masks via **morphological erosion/dilation of the predicted mask** (rather than only varying a probability threshold) and calibrates the shrink/grow parameter with conformal risk control to guarantee the predicted mask covers the true mask (or bounds a false-negative rate) at a chosen level $\alpha$ — this maps almost one-to-one onto the project's binary change-mask setting and is the single best template to adapt.
- **"Controlling False Positives in Image Segmentation via Conformal Prediction," arXiv:2511.15406** — directly targets the false-positive side of the risk, which is the side of most practical concern for a reviewer tool (false alarms waste reviewer time; the brief's own framing of "confidence gating protects real changes" implies controlling false negatives on true changes is the priority, but a conformal *false-positive* guarantee is the natural complementary deliverable for the "reduce search time" framing — most gain comes from suppressing confident false positives without losing true changes).

### 5.3 The recipe for this project, concretely

1. Fix a risk you want a guarantee on — most natural choice: **false-negative rate on changed pixels** (or equivalently, $1-$recall on the change class), since missing a real change is the costlier error for a reviewer aid than a false alarm.
2. Hold out a calibration set (separate from train/val/test — can be a slice of validation, but must not be reused for threshold tuning in §7 or the model will be double-dipping the same data for two different guarantees).
3. Define a nested family of prediction sets indexed by one scalar $\lambda$ — either (a) probability threshold sweep (mask = $\{p_{ij}>\lambda\}$, decreasing $\lambda$ grows the mask and lowers false negatives monotonically — satisfies the required monotonicity), or (b) morphological dilation of the thresholded mask by $\lambda$ pixels (Mossina & Friedrich's approach — monotone by construction, and often more label-efficient because it grows contiguous regions rather than just admitting more scattered low-confidence pixels).
4. Compute $\hat\lambda$ via the Conformal Risk Control formula above (or full RCPS with a concentration bound if a high-probability, not just in-expectation, guarantee is wanted for the final report's headline claim).
5. Report: "at $\lambda=\hat\lambda$, the true positive coverage of changed regions is guaranteed to be $\ge 1-\alpha$ on data exchangeable with the calibration set" — this is a *much stronger, more publishable* statement than an ECE number, because it is a finite-sample distribution-free guarantee rather than an average-case diagnostic.

### 5.4 Feasibility judgment: realistic semester scope, or stretch?

**Verdict: realistic as a scoped stretch goal, not as a core deliverable alongside everything else in this brief.** Reasoning: the *statistical* machinery (steps 1–4 above) is genuinely light — a few dozen lines of NumPy/PyTorch given predictions and a calibration set, no retraining, no new architecture, and reference code exists (`github.com/aangelopoulos/conformal-risk`, and the Mossina et al. group's `deel-ai-papers/conformal-segmentation` repo on GitHub is a CVPR-W 2024 companion implementation). The engineering risk is entirely in (a) correctly defining a monotone loss/nested-set family matched to the binary change-detection setting (morphological approach is the safer template to copy since it's already been done for binary masks) and (b) having a clean, sufficiently large, correctly-held-out calibration split — with limited labeled pairs in a student project, carving out yet another disjoint split (beyond train/val/test/threshold-calibration) is the real constraint, not the math. **Recommendation**: budget it as a stretch goal to attempt only after the MVP stack (§9) is working and validated, using it as the capstone "distribution-free guarantee" result for the final report rather than the backbone of the confidence-gating system, and reuse the same calibration split as the operating-point selection in §3.4 and threshold selection in §7 if a separate split truly cannot be spared (documenting that reuse as a limitation).

## 6. Losses for imbalanced binary segmentation and their calibration effects

### 6.1 Loss formulas

- **Weighted BCE**: $\mathcal L = -\frac1N\sum_i\big[w_1\, y_i\log \hat p_i + w_0\,(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big]$, with $w_1 \gg w_0$ (e.g. $w_1{=}N_{\text{neg}}/N_{\text{pos}}$ or a milder $\sqrt{\cdot}$/capped version to avoid destabilizing training under 2–5% prevalence).
- **Dice loss**: $\mathcal L_{\text{Dice}} = 1 - \dfrac{2\sum_i p_i g_i + \epsilon}{\sum_i p_i + \sum_i g_i + \epsilon}$ — a *global*, batch/image-level overlap measure, insensitive to the absolute pixel count of each class, which is exactly why it's attractive under imbalance (it doesn't let the easy majority class dominate the gradient the way plain BCE does) — but this same global, ratio-based structure is the documented root cause of the calibration problem below.
- **Tversky loss** (Salehi et al. 2017, arXiv:1706.05721): generalizes Dice with independent penalties on FP/FN, $\mathrm{TI} = \dfrac{TP}{TP+\alpha\,FP+\beta\,FN}$, $\mathcal L_{\text{Tversky}}=1-\mathrm{TI}$; setting $\beta>\alpha$ (commonly $\alpha{=}0.3,\beta{=}0.7$) trades precision for recall — directly usable to bias the network toward not missing real changes, complementary to the "protect real changes" framing in the proposal.
- **Focal loss** (Lin et al., ICCV 2017, arXiv:1708.02002): $\mathrm{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t(1-p_t)^\gamma\log(p_t)$ ($\gamma{=}2$ typical), down-weights already-easy (high-$p_t$) examples so the loss concentrates on hard/rare examples — a per-pixel, local reweighting, unlike Dice's global ratio.
- **Lovász-Softmax** (Berman, Triki, Blaschko, CVPR 2018, arXiv:1705.08790): a convex surrogate for the (non-differentiable) IoU/Jaccard loss via the Lovász extension of submodular set functions; directly optimizes an IoU-like objective rather than approximating it through Dice.

### 6.2 The calibration problem — flagged clearly for this project's planned BCE+Dice loss

**This is a direct, evidence-backed flag on the proposal's stated loss choice.** Two independent literatures converge on the same conclusion for Dice:

1. **Mehrtash, Wells, Tempany, Abolmaesumi, Kapur, "Confidence Calibration and Predictive Uncertainty Estimation for Deep Medical Image Segmentation," IEEE TMI 39(12), 2020 (arXiv:1911.13273)**: systematically compares cross-entropy vs. Dice-loss-trained FCNs and finds Dice-trained networks are **more overconfident / worse calibrated** than CE-trained ones, and proposes ensembling as a partial remedy — directly reusing the deep-ensemble machinery from §1.
2. **Yeung et al., "Calibrating the Dice Loss to Handle Neural Network Overconfidence for Biomedical Image Segmentation" (arXiv:2111.00528, published in J. Digital Imaging 2023)**: states plainly that "the DSC loss is poorly calibrated, resulting in overconfident predictions," and proposes a **DSC++ loss** that selectively down-weights the penalty on overconfident-incorrect predictions, showing significantly improved calibration over vanilla Dice across six biomedical benchmarks (2D binary and 3D multi-class) while preserving segmentation accuracy.

The mechanism in both papers is the same intuition worth stating in the report: Dice is a **global, ratio-based objective computed over the whole image/batch**, so it has no per-pixel notion of "how confident should this individual pixel's probability be" the way cross-entropy does — pushing the ratio toward 1 is compatible with (and empirically tends to produce) individual pixel probabilities saturated toward 0 or 1, i.e. overconfidence, especially once the changed-region overlap is already good and the optimizer keeps pushing.

**Focal loss is the interesting counter-example, and the report should present the nuance rather than a blanket "these all hurt calibration" claim**: Mukhoti et al., "Calibrating Deep Neural Networks using Focal Loss," NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv:2002.09437) show that, contrary to the naive expectation that down-weighting easy examples would sharpen (and thus worsen) confidence, focal loss produces networks that are **already well-calibrated** relative to cross-entropy, and combining it with temperature scaling gives state-of-the-art calibration — their explanation is that focal loss implicitly regularizes the predictive entropy, which offsets the overfitting-driven overconfidence that plain CE accumulates on unseen data. This means the calibration risk in the project's stack is attributable specifically to **the Dice term**, not to imbalance-handling losses in general — a useful, precise claim for the report rather than an overgeneralization.

Since BCE+Dice is the project's already-planned combination, the practical upshot: **weighted BCE alone is comparatively well-behaved for calibration; adding the Dice term is what introduces the documented overconfidence risk.** Two concrete, cheap fixes, both compatible with keeping BCE+Dice for its (real, needed) benefit on the 2–5%-prevalence overlap metric:

- **Fix A (near-zero cost, recommended default): post-hoc temperature scaling fit on validation data**, and — given §4's finding that a single global $T$ fit on clean data won't transfer to nuisance-shifted video frames — fit it specifically on a shift-representative validation slice, or fit a **class-wise/two-parameter variant** (separate scalars for changed vs. unchanged logit regions) if a single global $T$ under-corrects one class.
- **Fix B (moderate cost, calibration-aware auxiliary term): add a differentiable calibration-regularizing term to the loss** rather than only Dice+BCE — e.g., the **Average Calibration Error (ACE)**-style differentiable loss surfaced in §2 ("Average Calibration Losses for Reliable Uncertainty in Medical Image Segmentation," arXiv:2506.03942, and the margin-based label-smoothing constrained-optimization framing in Larrazabal et al., "Calibrating Segmentation Networks with Margin-based Label Smoothing," Medical Image Analysis 2023, arXiv:2209.09641 — the latter frames Dice/CE-style calibration losses as approximate equality constraints on logit margins and proposes a controllable-margin inequality-constraint generalization that improves calibration *and* preserves/improves accuracy, code available). Fix B is more work (a new loss term to implement and a weighting hyperparameter to tune) but addresses the miscalibration at training time rather than papering over it post-hoc, and Mehrtash et al.'s ensembling remedy is a third option that is "free" if the team is already doing snapshot ensembles for §1.

**Recommendation for the report:** implement Fix A unconditionally (cheap, ~1 hour), attempt Fix B only if reliability diagrams after Fix A still show a visible gap specifically on changed-class pixels, and cite the DSC++/Mehrtash/Mukhoti trio together as the evidentiary basis for flagging Dice (not BCE, not focal) as the calibration-risk term in the planned loss.

## 7. Threshold selection

Segmentation networks output probabilities; converting to a binary change mask requires a threshold $\tau$, and — separately — the confidence gate needs its own threshold(s) (§3). These interact and must be kept conceptually and operationally distinct, but tuned in a specific order:

1. **Fix $\tau$ for the mask on validation scenes only**, never on test scenes, exactly as emphasized in §3.4 and §5.3 — this is standard practice across the threshold-tuning literature (the "threshold-moving" technique for imbalanced classification: sweep $\tau$ over the validation precision–recall curve and pick the F1-maximizing point, or a **precision-constrained** point — e.g. "highest recall subject to precision $\ge 0.8$" — if the report wants to argue for a specific reviewer-workload trade-off rather than a symmetric F1 optimum). Under 2–5% positive prevalence, $\tau{=}0.5$ is very unlikely to be F1-optimal; expect the optimum closer to the base rate or lower, and always report the chosen $\tau$ and the curve it was chosen from, not just the resulting F1.
2. **F1-optimal vs. precision-constrained** is a framing choice tied to the reviewer-time-savings argument: F1-optimal balances the two error types symmetrically, while a precision-constrained threshold (fix a minimum acceptable false-alarm rate, maximize recall subject to it) is usually the more defensible choice for a *reviewer aid*, since the whole point of the tool is to not waste the human's time on false alarms while still surfacing real changes — this should be stated explicitly as a design choice, with both curves reported so the tradeoff is visible.
3. **Threshold choice interacts with calibration and the confidence gate in a specific, exploitable way**: once $\tau$ is fixed, the confidence gate should ideally be evaluated *conditional on the decision at $\tau$* — i.e., the pair-level confidence score in §3.2 is most informative when computed from how far the relevant pixels' probabilities sit from $\tau$ (not from 0.5), since a probability of 0.6 is a confident "changed" call if $\tau{=}0.3$ but a low-margin call if $\tau{=}0.55$. Concretely, redefine the boundary-proximity term from §3.2's confidence-aware deferral policy as $c_{ij} = 2|\hat p_{ij}-\tau|$ (clipped to $[0,1]$ appropriately) rather than the generic $2|\hat p_{ij}-0.5|$, so the abstention mechanism is coupled to the actual operating point rather than an arbitrary midpoint that may not match the chosen decision threshold at all.
4. **Order of operations to avoid data leakage across all of §3/§5/§7**: (a) train on train split; (b) pick $\tau$ on validation split; (c) pick the confidence-gate operating point on the *same* or a disjoint validation split (document which, and prefer disjoint if labeled data allows); (d) if attempting conformal calibration (§5), use a third disjoint calibration split, or explicitly document reuse as a limitation; (e) report all final numbers — Dice/IoU at $\tau$, ECE/Brier, RC curve and AURC/E-AURC, and (if attempted) the conformal guarantee — only on the held-out test split, touched exactly once.
